\begin{abstract}
This report presents a comprehensive implementation and evaluation of deep learning models for medical image segmentation, specifically focusing on brain tissue segmentation using the IBSR (Internet Brain Segmentation Repository) dataset. We implement and compare two state-of-the-art architectures: the classical U-Net and an enhanced Attention U-Net. The U-Net architecture demonstrates excellent performance in medical image segmentation tasks due to its encoder-decoder structure with skip connections, which preserves fine-grained spatial information. The Attention U-Net extends this by incorporating attention gates in the skip connections, allowing the model to focus on relevant features and suppress irrelevant background information. Our implementation includes comprehensive data preprocessing, model training with appropriate loss functions (combined Dice and Cross-Entropy loss), and extensive evaluation using multiple metrics including Dice coefficient, Intersection over Union (IoU), and pixel accuracy. The results demonstrate the effectiveness of both architectures, with detailed analysis of model performance, error patterns, and visualization of predictions. This work provides a complete pipeline for medical image segmentation that can be adapted for various clinical applications.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Medical image segmentation is a critical task in computer-aided diagnosis and treatment planning. Accurate segmentation of anatomical structures from medical images enables precise quantification, disease monitoring, and surgical planning. Among various medical imaging modalities, brain MRI segmentation presents unique challenges due to the complex anatomical structures, intensity inhomogeneities, and the need for pixel-level precision.

This assignment focuses on implementing and evaluating deep learning models for brain tissue segmentation using the IBSR dataset. The primary objectives are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Implement a U-Net architecture for medical image segmentation
    \item Extend the U-Net with attention mechanisms (Attention U-Net)
    \item Develop a comprehensive data preprocessing pipeline
    \item Train and evaluate models using appropriate loss functions and metrics
    \item Conduct thorough analysis and visualization of results
    \item Compare the performance of different architectures
\end{itemize}

The U-Net architecture, originally proposed by Ronneberger et al. \cite{ronneberger2015u}, has become the de facto standard for medical image segmentation due to its ability to capture both local and global context through its symmetric encoder-decoder structure with skip connections. The Attention U-Net, introduced by Oktay et al. \cite{oktay2018attention}, enhances this architecture by incorporating attention gates that learn to focus on relevant anatomical structures while suppressing irrelevant background.

\section{Related Work}

Medical image segmentation has evolved significantly with the advent of deep learning. This section provides a comprehensive overview of the evolution of segmentation methods, from traditional approaches to modern deep learning architectures.

\subsection{Traditional Segmentation Methods}

Before the deep learning era, medical image segmentation relied on traditional computer vision techniques:

\subsubsection{Thresholding Methods}
Simple thresholding techniques segment images based on intensity values. While computationally efficient, they fail to capture complex anatomical structures and are sensitive to intensity inhomogeneities common in medical images.

\subsubsection{Region Growing}
Region growing methods start from seed points and iteratively add neighboring pixels based on similarity criteria. These methods require manual seed selection and are sensitive to noise.

\subsubsection{Active Contours}
Active contour models (snakes) evolve curves to fit object boundaries. While effective for smooth boundaries, they struggle with complex topologies and require careful initialization.

\subsubsection{Graph-Based Methods}
Graph cuts and random walker algorithms formulate segmentation as graph partitioning problems. These methods can incorporate shape priors but are computationally expensive for high-resolution images.

\subsection{Deep Learning for Medical Image Segmentation}

The introduction of deep learning revolutionized medical image segmentation. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have shown remarkable success in various medical imaging tasks.

\subsubsection{Fully Convolutional Networks (FCNs)}
Long et al. \cite{long2015fcn} introduced Fully Convolutional Networks, replacing fully connected layers with convolutional layers to enable dense prediction. FCNs can handle arbitrary input sizes but lose spatial resolution through pooling operations.

\subsubsection{U-Net Architecture}
The U-Net architecture, proposed by Ronneberger et al. \cite{ronneberger2015u}, specifically designed for biomedical image segmentation, has achieved state-of-the-art results in numerous challenges including cell tracking, organ segmentation, and lesion detection. The key innovation of U-Net lies in its symmetric encoder-decoder architecture with skip connections, which allows the network to combine low-level features (fine details) with high-level features (semantic information). This design is particularly effective for medical images where precise boundary localization is crucial.

\subsubsection{V-Net and 3D Extensions}
Milletari et al. \cite{milletari2016vnet} extended U-Net to 3D volumes with V-Net, using 3D convolutions and Dice loss. This enables direct processing of 3D medical volumes without slice-by-slice processing.

\subsubsection{DeepLab and Atrous Convolutions}
Chen et al. \cite{chen2017deeplab} introduced atrous (dilated) convolutions to increase receptive field without losing resolution. DeepLab variants have shown excellent performance in various segmentation tasks.

\subsection{Attention Mechanisms in Medical Imaging}

Attention mechanisms, originally developed for natural language processing, have been successfully adapted for computer vision tasks. In medical image segmentation, attention gates help the model focus on relevant anatomical structures, improving segmentation accuracy especially in regions with complex boundaries or low contrast.

\subsubsection{Attention U-Net}
Oktay et al. \cite{oktay2018attention} introduced Attention U-Net, incorporating attention gates in skip connections. This allows the model to learn which encoder features are most relevant, suppressing irrelevant background information.

\subsubsection{Self-Attention and Transformers}
Recent work has explored self-attention mechanisms and transformer architectures for medical image segmentation, showing promising results in capturing long-range dependencies.

\subsection{Recent Advances}

\subsubsection{Multi-Scale Architectures}
Modern architectures incorporate multi-scale feature extraction to capture information at different resolutions, improving segmentation of objects with varying sizes.

\subsubsection{Ensemble Methods}
Combining predictions from multiple models has shown to improve robustness and accuracy, though at the cost of increased computational requirements.

\subsubsection{Transfer Learning}
Pre-trained models on large datasets (e.g., ImageNet) are fine-tuned for medical tasks, reducing training time and improving performance, especially with limited medical data.

\section{Dataset and Preprocessing}

\subsection{Dataset Description}

The IBSR (Internet Brain Segmentation Repository) dataset consists of 3D brain MRI volumes with corresponding manual segmentations. The dataset is commonly used for brain tissue segmentation tasks, where the goal is to classify each voxel into different tissue types (e.g., white matter, gray matter, cerebrospinal fluid).

For this assignment, we process the 3D volumes by extracting 2D slices along three orthogonal axes (axial, coronal, and sagittal), creating a diverse set of training samples. Each volume is processed to extract patches of size 128×128 pixels, which are then padded to 256×256 pixels to ensure consistent input dimensions.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing Pipeline}

The data preprocessing pipeline consists of several key steps:

\subsubsection{Slice Extraction}
Slices are extracted from 3D volumes along three axes (axis 0, 1, and 2) to maximize data diversity. For each axis:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Slices are extracted starting from index 10 to avoid edge artifacts
    \item A stride of 3 is used to ensure diversity while maintaining computational efficiency
    \item Up to 48 slices are extracted per axis per volume
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Rotation and Padding}
Each extracted slice undergoes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item 90-degree rotation to standardize orientation
    \item Padding to target dimensions (256×256) using zero-padding
    \item Centered padding to preserve spatial relationships
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Patch Extraction}
From each 256×256 padded slice, four non-overlapping patches of size 128×128 are extracted:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Top-left patch: coordinates (0, 0)
    \item Top-right patch: coordinates (0, 128)
    \item Bottom-left patch: coordinates (128, 0)
    \item Bottom-right patch: coordinates (128, 128)
\end{itemize}

This approach significantly augments the dataset, creating multiple training samples from each volume while maintaining spatial coherence.

\subsubsection{Dataset Split}
The dataset is split into:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Training set: 70\% of volumes
    \item Validation set: 15\% of volumes
    \item Test set: 15\% of volumes
\end{itemize}

This split ensures sufficient training data while maintaining separate validation and test sets for unbiased evaluation.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{U-Net Architecture}

The U-Net architecture consists of two main parts: an encoder (contracting path) and a decoder (expanding path), connected by skip connections.

\subsubsection{Encoder (Contracting Path)}
The encoder follows a typical CNN architecture, progressively reducing spatial dimensions while increasing feature channels:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Input}: Single-channel image (128×128)
    \item \textbf{Block 1}: 64 channels, maintains spatial dimensions
    \item \textbf{Block 2}: 128 channels, spatial dimensions halved
    \item \textbf{Block 3}: 256 channels, spatial dimensions halved again
    \item \textbf{Block 4}: 512 channels, further spatial reduction
    \item \textbf{Bottleneck}: 1024 channels, minimum spatial dimensions
\end{itemize}

Each block consists of two 3×3 convolutions, each followed by batch normalization and ReLU activation. Max pooling with 2×2 kernel is used for downsampling.

\subsubsection{Decoder (Expanding Path)}
The decoder mirrors the encoder structure, progressively increasing spatial dimensions while reducing channels:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Upsampling using transposed convolution or bilinear interpolation
    \item Concatenation with corresponding encoder features via skip connections
    \item Two 3×3 convolutions with batch normalization and ReLU
    \item Final 1×1 convolution to produce class predictions
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Skip Connections}
Skip connections are the key innovation of U-Net. They concatenate features from the encoder to the decoder at corresponding resolution levels, allowing the network to:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Preserve fine-grained spatial information}: Low-level features from early encoder layers contain detailed spatial information about edges, textures, and boundaries. Skip connections preserve this information that would otherwise be lost through pooling operations.
    \item \textbf{Combine low-level and high-level features}: The decoder combines high-level semantic features (from deeper layers) with low-level spatial features (from skip connections), enabling both semantic understanding and precise localization.
    \item \textbf{Improve boundary localization accuracy}: The combination of semantic and spatial information allows the network to accurately localize object boundaries, which is crucial for medical image segmentation.
    \item \textbf{Enable gradient flow}: Skip connections provide direct paths for gradients to flow from decoder to encoder, facilitating training of deep networks.
\end{itemize}

The skip connection operation can be mathematically described as:
\begin{equation}
x_{\text{decoder}}^{(l)} = \text{Concat}(\text{Upsample}(x_{\text{decoder}}^{(l+1)}), x_{\text{encoder}}^{(L-l)})
\end{equation}

where $l$ is the decoder layer index, $L$ is the total number of encoder/decoder levels, and Concat denotes channel-wise concatenation.

\subsection{Attention U-Net Architecture}

The Attention U-Net extends the standard U-Net by incorporating attention gates in the skip connections. This allows the model to learn which encoder features are most relevant for the segmentation task.

\subsubsection{Attention Gate Mechanism}
The attention gate consists of:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Gating signal} ($g$): Features from the decoder (coarser resolution)
    \item \textbf{Input features} ($x$): Features from the encoder (finer resolution)
    \item \textbf{Attention coefficients} ($\alpha$): Learned weights indicating feature relevance
\end{enumerate}

The attention coefficients are computed as:
\begin{equation}
\alpha = \sigma(W_\psi^T \text{ReLU}(W_g^T g + W_x^T x))
\end{equation}

where $W_g$, $W_x$, and $W_\psi$ are learned weight matrices, and $\sigma$ is the sigmoid activation function.

\subsubsection{Attention Gate Architecture}
Each attention gate includes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Two 1×1 convolutions to transform gating signal and input features
    \item Element-wise addition of transformed features
    \item ReLU activation
    \item 1×1 convolution to produce attention coefficients
    \item Sigmoid activation to normalize coefficients to [0, 1]
\end{itemize}

The attention-weighted features are then concatenated with the upsampled decoder features, allowing the model to focus on relevant anatomical structures.

\subsection{Loss Functions}

Medical image segmentation faces the challenge of class imbalance, where background pixels typically outnumber foreground pixels. Additionally, the task requires precise boundary localization, making standard classification losses suboptimal. To address these challenges, we use a combined loss function that leverages the strengths of multiple loss components.

\subsubsection{Loss Function Design Rationale}

The choice of loss function significantly impacts segmentation performance. We consider several factors:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Class imbalance}: Background pixels often dominate, requiring loss functions that don't bias toward majority classes
    \item \textbf{Boundary precision}: Medical applications require accurate boundary localization, not just pixel-wise accuracy
    \item \textbf{Gradient stability}: The loss function should provide stable gradients for effective training
    \item \textbf{Computational efficiency}: The loss should be computationally efficient for large images
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Dice Loss}
The Dice coefficient measures the overlap between predicted and ground truth segmentations:
\begin{equation}
\text{Dice} = \frac{2|X \cap Y|}{|X| + |Y|} = \frac{2TP}{2TP + FP + FN}
\end{equation}

Dice loss is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Dice}} = 1 - \frac{2\sum_{i} p_i y_i + \epsilon}{\sum_{i} p_i + \sum_{i} y_i + \epsilon}
\end{equation}

where $p_i$ are predicted probabilities, $y_i$ are ground truth labels, and $\epsilon$ is a smoothing term to avoid division by zero.

\subsubsection{Cross-Entropy Loss}
Standard cross-entropy loss provides pixel-wise classification:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} = -\sum_{i} y_i \log(p_i)
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Combined Loss}
We combine both losses to leverage their complementary strengths:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Total}} = \lambda_{\text{Dice}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{Dice}} + \lambda_{\text{CE}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}
\end{equation}

where $\lambda_{\text{Dice}} = 0.5$ and $\lambda_{\text{CE}} = 0.5$ in our implementation. The Dice loss handles class imbalance, while cross-entropy provides stable gradients.

\subsubsection{Loss Function Analysis}

The combined loss function offers several advantages:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dice loss component}: Directly optimizes the Dice coefficient, which is a primary evaluation metric. It naturally handles class imbalance by normalizing by the sum of predictions and ground truth.
    \item \textbf{Cross-entropy component}: Provides pixel-wise supervision and stable gradients, especially in early training when predictions are far from ground truth.
    \item \textbf{Complementary behavior}: Dice loss focuses on overlap (region-based), while cross-entropy focuses on pixel-wise accuracy. Their combination ensures both global and local accuracy.
    \item \textbf{Hyperparameter sensitivity}: The weights $\lambda_{\text{Dice}}$ and $\lambda_{\text{CE}}$ can be tuned based on the specific task and dataset characteristics.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Alternative Loss Functions}

Other loss functions considered but not implemented include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Focal Loss}: Addresses class imbalance by down-weighting easy examples and focusing on hard examples. However, it requires additional hyperparameter tuning.
    \item \textbf{Tversky Loss}: Generalizes Dice loss with asymmetric penalties for false positives and false negatives. Useful when precision and recall have different importance.
    \item \textbf{Boundary Loss}: Explicitly penalizes boundary errors, which could be beneficial for precise boundary localization.
    \item \textbf{Combo Loss}: Combines Dice and cross-entropy with additional terms for boundary preservation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

We evaluate model performance using multiple metrics:

\subsubsection{Dice Coefficient}
The Dice coefficient (also called F1 score) measures segmentation overlap:
\begin{equation}
\text{Dice} = \frac{2|P \cap G|}{|P| + |G|}
\end{equation}
where $P$ is the predicted segmentation and $G$ is the ground truth.

\subsubsection{Intersection over Union (IoU)}
IoU (also called Jaccard index) measures the ratio of intersection to union:
\begin{equation}
\text{IoU} = \frac{|P \cap G|}{|P \cup G|} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP + FN}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Pixel Accuracy}
Pixel accuracy measures the fraction of correctly classified pixels:
\begin{equation}
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Per-Class Metrics}
We also compute Dice and IoU scores for each class separately to identify which tissue types are easier or harder to segment.

    \item \textbf{Epsilon for Adam}: $1 \times 10^{-8}$ (numerical stability)

\subsubsection{Hyperparameter Selection}

Hyperparameters were selected through a combination of literature review, empirical testing, and consideration of computational constraints:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Learning rate}: Started with $1 \times 10^{-3}$ but reduced to $1 \times 10^{-4}$ for more stable training. Too high learning rates caused training instability, while too low rates resulted in slow convergence.
    \item \textbf{Batch size}: Tested batch sizes of 8, 16, and 32. Batch size 16 provided good balance between memory usage and gradient estimation quality.
    \item \textbf{Weight decay}: Tested values from $1 \times 10^{-6}$ to $1 \times 10^{-4}$. $1 \times 10^{-5}$ provided good regularization without excessive penalty on model capacity.
    \item \textbf{Early stopping patience}: Set to 10 epochs based on observed convergence patterns. Models typically converged within 30-40 epochs, so patience of 10 prevents unnecessary training.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Training Strategy}

The training strategy is designed to ensure robust learning, prevent overfitting, and enable efficient model selection:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Early stopping}: Monitors validation Dice score with patience of 10 epochs. Training stops if validation performance doesn't improve for 10 consecutive epochs, preventing overfitting and saving computational resources.
    \item \textbf{Model checkpointing}: Saves the best model based on validation Dice score. This ensures we retain the model with best generalization performance, not just the final model which may have overfitted.
    \item \textbf{Gradient clipping}: Applied with maximum gradient norm of 1.0 to prevent exploding gradients, which can occur in deep networks or with unstable loss functions.
    \item \textbf{Mixed precision training}: Uses FP16 precision where possible to accelerate training and reduce memory usage, while maintaining FP32 precision for loss computation to ensure numerical stability.
    \item \textbf{Validation frequency}: Validates after each epoch to closely monitor training progress and enable early stopping.
    \item \textbf{Learning rate warmup}: Not implemented in current version, but could be added for more stable training in the first few epochs.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Training Algorithm}

The training process follows Algorithm \ref{alg:training}:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{U-Net Training Algorithm}
\label{alg:training}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N$, model $f_\theta$, loss function $\mathcal{L}$, optimizer $\text{Opt}$
\ENSURE Trained model $f_{\theta^*}$
\STATE Initialize model parameters $\theta$ randomly
\STATE Split dataset into train $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$, validation $\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}$, test $\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}$
\STATE Initialize best validation Dice score $d_{\text{best}} = 0$
\STATE Initialize patience counter $p = 0$
\FOR{epoch $e = 1$ to $E_{\text{max}}$}
    \STATE Set model to training mode
    \FOR{each batch $(x_b, y_b) \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$}
        \STATE Compute predictions: $\hat{y}_b = f_\theta(x_b)$
        \STATE Compute loss: $\ell = \mathcal{L}(\hat{y}_b, y_b)$
        \STATE Compute gradients: $\nabla_\theta \ell$
        \STATE Clip gradients if $||\nabla_\theta \ell|| > \tau$
        \STATE Update parameters: $\theta \leftarrow \text{Opt}(\theta, \nabla_\theta \ell)$
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE Set model to evaluation mode
    \STATE Compute validation Dice score $d_{\text{val}}$ on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}$
    \IF{$d_{\text{val}} > d_{\text{best}}$}
        \STATE $d_{\text{best}} \leftarrow d_{\text{val}}$
        \STATE Save model checkpoint: $f_{\theta^*} = f_\theta$
        \STATE $p \leftarrow 0$
    \ELSE
        \STATE $p \leftarrow p + 1$
        \IF{$p \geq P_{\text{patience}}$}
            \STATE Break (early stopping)
        \ENDIF
    \ENDIF
    \STATE Update learning rate scheduler based on validation loss
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $f_{\theta^*}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

def get_slice_data(volume_data, seg_data, axis, slice_idx):
    """
    Extract 2D slice from 3D volume along specified axis.
    
    Args:
        volume_data: 3D numpy array of volume data
        seg_data: 3D numpy array of segmentation data
        axis: Axis along which to extract slice (0, 1, or 2)
        slice_idx: Index of slice to extract
    
    Returns:
        vol_slice_rotated: Rotated volume slice
        seg_slice_rotated: Rotated segmentation slice
    """
    if axis == 0:
        vol_slice = volume_data[slice_idx, :, :]
        seg_slice = seg_data[slice_idx, :, :]
    elif axis == 1:
        vol_slice = volume_data[:, slice_idx, :]
        seg_slice = seg_data[:, slice_idx, :]
    else:
        vol_slice = volume_data[:, :, slice_idx]
        seg_slice = seg_data[:, :, slice_idx]
    
    # Remove singleton dimensions
    vol_slice = np.squeeze(vol_slice)
    seg_slice = np.squeeze(seg_slice)
    
    # Rotate 90 degrees for standardization
    vol_slice_rotated = np.rot90(vol_slice, k=1)
    seg_slice_rotated = np.rot90(seg_slice, k=1)
    
    return vol_slice_rotated, seg_slice_rotated
\end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection{Padding Function}
The padding function ensures consistent input dimensions:
\begin{lstlisting}
def pad_slice(vol_slice, seg_slice, target_dims=(256, 256)):
    """
    Pad slice to target dimensions with centered padding.
    
    Args:
        vol_slice: Volume slice to pad
        seg_slice: Segmentation slice to pad
        target_dims: Target dimensions (height, width)
    
    Returns:
        vol_padded: Padded volume slice
        seg_padded: Padded segmentation slice
    """
    h, w = vol_slice.shape
    th, tw = target_dims

    # Calculate padding amounts (centered)
    pad_h_top = (th - h) // 2
    pad_h_bottom = th - h - pad_h_top
    pad_w_left = (tw - w) // 2
    pad_w_right = tw - w - pad_w_left

    # Create padding tuple
    padding = ((max(0, pad_h_top), max(0, pad_h_bottom)), 
               (max(0, pad_w_left), max(0, pad_w_right)))

    # Apply zero-padding
    vol_padded = np.pad(vol_slice, padding, mode='constant', constant_values=0)
    seg_padded = np.pad(seg_slice, padding, mode='constant', constant_values=0)
    
    # Ensure exact target dimensions
    vol_padded = vol_padded[:th, :tw]
    seg_padded = seg_padded[:th, :tw]

    return vol_padded, seg_padded
\end{lstlisting}

class UNet(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, n_channels, n_classes, bilinear=False):
        super(UNet, self).__init__()
        self.n_channels = n_channels
        self.n_classes = n_classes
        self.bilinear = bilinear

        self.inc = DoubleConv(n_channels, 64)
        self.down1 = Down(64, 128)
        self.down2 = Down(128, 256)
        self.down3 = Down(256, 512)
        factor = 2 if bilinear else 1
        self.down4 = Down(512, 1024 // factor)
        self.up1 = Up(1024, 512 // factor, bilinear)
        self.up2 = Up(512, 256 // factor, bilinear)
        self.up3 = Up(256, 128 // factor, bilinear)
        self.up4 = Up(128, 64, bilinear)
        self.outc = OutConv(64, n_classes)

    def forward(self, x):
        x1 = self.inc(x)
        x2 = self.down1(x1)
        x3 = self.down2(x2)
        x4 = self.down3(x3)
        x5 = self.down4(x4)
        x = self.up1(x5, x4)
        x = self.up2(x, x3)
        x = self.up3(x, x2)
        x = self.up4(x, x1)
        logits = self.outc(x)
        return logits
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Loss Function}

\subsubsection{Dice Loss Implementation}
\begin{lstlisting}
class DiceLoss(nn.Module):
    """
    Dice Loss for handling class imbalance in segmentation.
    """
    def __init__(self, smooth=1.0):
        super(DiceLoss, self).__init__()
        self.smooth = smooth

    def forward(self, inputs, targets):
        # Apply softmax to get probabilities
        inputs = F.softmax(inputs, dim=1)
        
        # One-hot encode targets
        num_classes = inputs.shape[1]
        targets_one_hot = F.one_hot(targets, num_classes).permute(0, 3, 1, 2).float()
        
        # Flatten tensors
        inputs_flat = inputs.view(inputs.size(0), inputs.size(1), -1)
        targets_flat = targets_one_hot.view(targets_one_hot.size(0), 
                                           targets_one_hot.size(1), -1)
        
        # Calculate Dice coefficient for each class
        intersection = (inputs_flat * targets_flat).sum(dim=2)
        union = inputs_flat.sum(dim=2) + targets_flat.sum(dim=2)
        
        dice = (2. * intersection + self.smooth) / (union + self.smooth)
        dice_loss = 1 - dice.mean()
        
        return dice_loss
\end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection{Combined Loss Implementation}
\begin{lstlisting}
class CombinedLoss(nn.Module):
    """
    Combined Dice and Cross-Entropy Loss.
    Combines benefits of both loss functions.
    """
    def __init__(self, dice_weight=0.5, ce_weight=0.5, smooth=1.0):
        super(CombinedLoss, self).__init__()
        self.dice_weight = dice_weight
        self.ce_weight = ce_weight
        self.dice_loss = DiceLoss(smooth=smooth)
        self.ce_loss = nn.CrossEntropyLoss()

    def forward(self, inputs, targets):
        dice = self.dice_loss(inputs, targets)
        ce = self.ce_loss(inputs, targets)
        return self.dice_weight * dice + self.ce_weight * ce
\end{lstlisting}

def calculate_dice_score(pred, target, num_classes, smooth=1.0):
    """
    Calculate Dice score for each class.
    
    Args:
        pred: Predicted segmentation (B, H, W)
        target: Ground truth segmentation (B, H, W)
        num_classes: Number of classes
        smooth: Smoothing factor
    
    Returns:
        dice: Dice scores for each class
    """
    pred_one_hot = F.one_hot(pred, num_classes).permute(0, 3, 1, 2).float()
    target_one_hot = F.one_hot(target, num_classes).permute(0, 3, 1, 2).float()
    
    pred_flat = pred_one_hot.view(pred_one_hot.size(0), pred_one_hot.size(1), -1)
    target_flat = target_one_hot.view(target_one_hot.size(0), target_one_hot.size(1), -1)
    
    intersection = (pred_flat * target_flat).sum(dim=2)
    union = pred_flat.sum(dim=2) + target_flat.sum(dim=2)
    
    dice = (2. * intersection + smooth) / (union + smooth)
    return dice.mean(dim=0)  # Average over batch, return per-class scores
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Error Analysis Details}

\subsection{Common Error Patterns}

Analysis of prediction errors reveals several common patterns:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Boundary Errors}: Most errors occur at tissue boundaries, especially in low-contrast regions
    \item \textbf{Partial Volume Effects}: Errors at boundaries between tissues due to mixed voxel intensities
    \item \textbf{Intensity Inhomogeneities}: Errors in regions with intensity variations not seen in training
    \item \textbf{Small Structures}: Difficulty segmenting small structures due to limited spatial context
    \item \textbf{Artifact Regions}: Errors near imaging artifacts or noise
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Error Mitigation Strategies}

Strategies to reduce errors:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Data Augmentation}: Increase diversity of training data
    \item \textbf{Post-processing}: Apply morphological operations to smooth boundaries
    \item \textbf{Ensemble Methods}: Combine multiple models to reduce errors
    \item \textbf{Active Learning}: Focus training on difficult cases
    \item \textbf{Multi-scale Training}: Train with multiple resolutions
\end{itemize}
